First trip to China

Set up your phone, payments & bookings before you fly.

China runs on apps and QR codes. Almost everything on this list is easy at home and painful after you land — so work through it in countdown order, from 30 days out to your first 72 hours.

18 steps · in order About 4 hours of setup, spread over a month For first-time visitors Free — no account, nothing to sign up for

Why the order matters

Three things only really work before departure: payment apps need passport verification on a normal connection, several booking systems verify foreign passports slowly, and Google services — Maps, Gmail, Play Store — do not load on mainland connections. Do those at home and the trip itself gets simple.

30days

30 days before — permission & accounts

The slow paperwork. Start it first because none of it is instant.

✓ Check how you'll enter: visa-free, 240-hour transit, or a visa20 min

Many nationalities can now enter visa-free for a short stay, and travellers from many countries can also use the 240-hour (10-day) visa-free transit route if flying in from one country and out to a third. Check which route fits your passport and your exact itinerary — the transit route has conditions on ports of entry and onward tickets.

Why first: if you do need a visa, processing time decides everything else. Confirm the current rules for your nationality on an official Chinese embassy or immigration source, not a blog.
✓ Create your Railway 12306 account and submit passport verification15 min + waiting

12306 is China's official rail booking system — nearly every train ticket starts here. Register with your passport details exactly as printed, then submit the identity verification straight away.

Why now: passport verification for foreign documents can be slow from abroad — sometimes days. Starting at 30 days out means it is done long before tickets for your travel dates go on sale.
✓ Book hotels that explicitly accept foreign guests — on a refundable rate30 min

When booking, check the listing states it accepts guests from outside mainland China, and choose a refundable rate while your plans are still moving. International booking platforms flag this clearly; small local listings often don't.

Good to know: hotels have not been allowed to refuse foreign guests since 2024 — but enforcement is uneven, especially at small or budget properties. A listing that says it accepts you, in writing, saves an awkward arrival.
21days

21 days before — the tickets that sell out

Major sights are real-name and timed. Your passport is your ticket.

✓ Book big sights under your passport number20 min

The Forbidden City and other headline sights sell real-name, timed-entry tickets tied to your passport — there is often no walk-up option, and popular slots go days ahead. Book each sight for a specific date and time slot, using the passport you will actually carry.

Carry the same passport. Entry staff check the document against the booking. A booking under an old or different passport number can mean no entry.
✓ Book your longest train legs as soon as sales open15 min

With your 12306 verification done, book intercity trains when your dates open up — holiday and weekend departures on popular routes sell out. Screenshot every booking; the details live in the app, but a screenshot works with no signal.
14days

14 days before — payments & data

The most important week. This is what makes China feel easy or impossible.

✓ Install Alipay and WeChat — and finish setup at home25 min

Download both apps, bind a foreign Visa or Mastercard, and complete the passport identity verification while you're still at home. Do a tiny test payment if you can. Alipay alone covers most visitors; WeChat Pay is the backup some smaller places prefer.

The fee rule: single transactions under ¥200 carry no fee on a foreign card; above ¥200, a 3% fee applies. Splitting a large bill into smaller payments where a shop allows it keeps you under the line.
✓ Buy and install an eSIM for data on arrival10 min

Buy a China data eSIM before you fly and install it at home — most activate automatically when you land. You need data working in the first ten minutes: for maps, your hotel address, and your payment apps. See Airalo in Book & buy.

Do it before departure: buying and installing an eSIM is far simpler on your home connection than on airport Wi-Fi with a queue behind you.
✓ Sort travel insurance10 min

Buy cover that includes medical care in China and trip disruption, and save the policy number and emergency contact offline in your phone — not just in your email. See SafetyWing in Book & buy.
7days

7 days before — go offline-ready

Assume your usual apps won't load. Prepare the replacements now.

✓ Download offline maps and apps before departure20 min

Google services do not load on mainland connections — that includes Google Maps and the Play Store, so anything you forget cannot be fetched later. Download offline map areas for every city you're visiting, plus a translation app with the Chinese language pack downloaded for offline use.
✓ Save every key address in Chinese characters15 min

Save your hotel and main destinations in Chinese characters — as notes and as screenshots. Taxi drivers and helpful strangers work from the Chinese address; an English or pinyin address often means nothing on the street. Your hotel's booking page or listing usually shows the Chinese address — copy it from there.

Make a “show the driver” note: hotel name + Chinese address + the hotel's phone number, in one screenshot. It solves most first-day transport problems.
✓ Screenshot your documents folder10 min

One offline folder: passport photo page, visa or visa-free basis, hotel confirmations, train bookings, insurance, and your Chinese address cards. If your phone dies or has no signal, a travelling companion's copy helps too.
3days

3 days before — final checks

Small things, done once, that remove arrival-day friction.

✓ Fill in the online arrival card before landing10 min

Complete the online arrival card in advance and save the confirmation. It is one less form, queue, and pen hunt between the plane and passport control.
✓ Get ¥300–500 in small cash notes as backup10 min

Carry ¥300–500 in small notes. Mobile payment covers nearly everything, but cash rescues you when a phone dies, a small vendor's code won't scan, or a card check fails. Small notes matter — a ¥100 note can be hard to break.
✓ Test everything once, on purpose10 min

Open Alipay and WeChat and confirm your card shows as active. Open 12306 and confirm you're logged in and verified. Check the eSIM is installed and ready to activate. Charge a power bank — your phone is now your wallet, ticket, map, and translator.
72hours

First 72 hours in China

You've landed. Four things to settle in the first three days.

✓ Turn on the eSIM and make one small payment5 min

Activate data as soon as you're off the plane, then buy something small — a water, a metro ride — with Alipay. Proving your payment setup works on a ¥5 purchase beats discovering a problem on a ¥500 one.
✓ Set up your transit code for the city you're in5 min

Metro and bus travel uses a separate transit code inside Alipay or WeChat, activated per city. Set it up at the hotel, not at the gate with a queue behind you. See the 3 QR codes below.
✓ If you're not in a hotel: register your stay within 24 hours30 min

Staying with friends, or in a rental? You must register your stay with the local police within 24 hours of arrival. Hotels do this for you automatically when you check in with your passport — one more reason a hotel is the simplest base for a first trip.
✓ Keep your passport on you for ticketed sights and trains—

Your bookings are tied to it, and stations and sights check it at entry. A photo of it is a useful backup, but it doesn't replace the document at a ticket gate.

The three QR codes that confuse everyone

China uses QR codes for almost everything, and the three you'll meet daily work in opposite directions. Mixing them up is the most common first-day stall — here's the difference.

You show it

1 · Pay code

Your personal payment barcode. The cashier scans you. Find it in Alipay or WeChat under Pay / Receive.

Used at: supermarkets, chain cafés, convenience stores — anywhere with a scanner at the till.

You scan it

2 · Scan-to-pay

The shop's code, printed on a card at the counter. You open Scan, point your phone at it, type the amount, confirm.

Used at: street stalls, small restaurants, market vendors. Also how you scan a menu to order in many restaurants.

You show it — at gates

3 · Transit code

A separate code just for metro and buses, under Transport in Alipay or WeChat. It is not your pay code.

Set it up once per city before your first ride, and hold it up to the gate scanner — fares are charged to your linked card automatically.
